Five countries, one river

upstream wants power · downstream wants water
Upstream states (Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan) sit on the reservoirs and want winter releases for electricity. Downstream states (Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan) want that same water held back for the summer growing season. That single clash is the real politics of Central Asian water. A good operator keeps everyone tolerably unhappy, not one country delighted and the rest ruined.

The two equations behind the machine ›

Everything you see is driven by two pieces of real physics and chemistry. This is a teaching model built on published-order numbers, not a validated forecast — but the tradeoff structure is real.

1 · Hydropower (physics)

E = ρ · g · h · η · V

Energy from released water: density ρ (1000 kg/m³) × gravity g (9.81 m/s²) × dam head h (Toktogul ≈ 215 m, Nurek ≈ 275 m) × efficiency η × released volume V. Nurek is the tallest dam on Earth, so each cubic metre falls further and makes more electricity. Releasing in winter gives upstream countries the power they need to survive the cold, but that water then misses the summer fields downstream.

2 · The reservoir balance (storage)

S(t+1) = clamp( S(t) + inflow − release , 0 , capacity )

A dam is a bank account for water. Hold some back this year (release under 100%) and you can survive a drought later — but if the reservoir overfills, it spills anyway. This is why Toktogul, a multi-year reservoir, is the single most fought-over object in the region.

3 · The Aral Sea (chemistry of a closed basin)

V(t+1) = V(t) + inflow_to_sea − evaporation( area )

The Aral has no outlet. Water only leaves by evaporating off its surface. When irrigation upstream steals the inflow, evaporation keeps removing water that nothing replaces, so the sea shrinks — and because the salt stays behind, what's left turns from a fishery into a brine. Between 1960 and 2010 the sea lost about 90% of its water. This model reproduces that when you run the Cotton machine preset.
